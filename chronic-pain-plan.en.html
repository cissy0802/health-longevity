MANAGEMENT PROGRAM

Chronic Pain Plan
Shoulder · Back · Knee

Three regional modules · 10–15 min a day · progress by criteria, not by week number · red-flag list included
This plan's stance: chronic pain (past three months) is usually not "something is broken" but reduced tissue tolerance plus a nervous system amplifying danger signals. That explains two confusing facts: degenerative findings on imaging are just as common in people with no pain, and plenty of people hurt for years with nothing visible on a scan. So the goal here is not to repair structure but to rebuild tolerance, a little at a time — controlled load, monitored pain, a sustainable pace. It does not replace diagnosis: read the red-flag list at the end first.
FOUNDATION
Three things that decide whether any of this works
Foundation 1 · The pain traffic light (your only intensity rule)
Pain during the exerciseWhat to do
0–3 (green)Safe. You may add a little next time
4–5 (amber)Acceptable, but don't add anything this session — hold
> 5, or not back to baseline next morning (red)Too much. Drop back to the previous dose — not to nothing
Some pain during exercise is allowed in chronic pain rehab; trials comparing pain-permitting protocols with strictly pain-free ones find them no worse and sometimes slightly better in the short term. There is only one real line: it must be back to baseline by the next morning. If it isn't, the dose was too big.
Foundation 2 · Progressive load, not rest
For chronic pain, complete rest is almost always wrong: tolerance keeps falling, ordinary life becomes more provocative, and the loop of "hurts so I move less, move less so it hurts" tightens. The direction is to push load up from whatever you can currently tolerate — even if that starting point is "push against a wall for 20 seconds". Adding roughly 10% a week is far safer than jumping to where you think you should be.
Foundation 3 · What imaging shows is often not what hurts
Imaging findingIn people with no symptoms
Disc degenerationAbout a third of people in their twenties, rising steadily with age to near-universal by 80
Rotator cuff tearsCommon past 60, and most of those people have never had shoulder pain
Meniscal tearsFound in roughly a fifth to a third of asymptomatic middle-aged and older adults
This doesn't make imaging useless — it rules serious things out. But for chronic shoulder, back or knee pain without red flags, early imaging doesn't improve outcomes and often makes them worse: seeing the words "degeneration" or "tear" makes people move less. Train for 6–12 weeks first, then decide is usually the better order.
MODULE 1 · SHOULDER
Shoulder · rotator-cuff related pain and "impingement"
StageWhatDoseMove on when
1 · Settle and switch onIsometric external rotation at a wall: upper arm at your side, elbow at 90°, back of the hand pressing outwards without moving30–45 s × 5, dailyNo flare afterwards, and you can get the arm to shoulder height in daily life
2 · Add loadBand external rotation + band rows (shoulder blade moves first)3 × 12–15 each, every other dayTwo straight weeks in the green, and you no longer avoid the end of the range
3 · Overhead and weightedWall slides → dumbbell press / lateral raise within a pain-free range, starting very light3 × 8–12, 2–3× per weekEveryday overhead tasks (hanging laundry, reaching a high shelf) are pain-free
Don't stop lifting the arm just because it hurts. The shoulder is unusually sensitive to immobility — a few weeks of avoidance and range starts disappearing, sometimes towards a frozen shoulder. Move it daily within the range that stays out of the red, and let the range grow as tolerance does.
On surgery: subacromial decompression for "impingement" showed no clinically important benefit over placebo surgery in high-quality trials, and no clear advantage over exercise rehabilitation. Putting three months of serious progressive loading before an operation is the evidence-supported order.
MODULE 2 · BACK
Back · chronic low back pain and the upper back
StageWhatDoseMove on when
1 · Get moving againWalking (the most underrated item here) + modified curl-up / side plank / bird dog20–30 min walking daily; 3 short holds of each, dailyYou can walk 30 minutes without a next-day flare
2 · Learn to use the hipsUnloaded hip hinge: back neutral, bend at the hip rather than rounding the spine3 × 10, every other dayIn a mirror or a video, the back stays long and the movement comes from the hip
3 · Lift things againLoaded hinge (kettlebell, bucket of water) → picking objects up off the floor3 × 8–10, 2× per week, +10% load weeklyYou can put a suitcase in the car without thinking about it
"Bending to lift wrecks your back" has hurt more people than it has helped. Spinal flexion is not an injury mechanism in itself — what relates to pain is load exceeding current tolerance. The goal isn't a permanently flat back, it's rebuilding tolerance to bending and lifting so ordinary movements stop being a minefield.
Bed rest is clearly harmful: more than a day or two of it lengthens recovery. Even in an acute flare, stay as active as tolerance allows.
MODULE 3 · KNEE
Knee · patellofemoral pain and knee osteoarthritis
StageWhatDoseMove on when
1 · Start isometricWall sit or Spanish squat (band behind the knees), torso upright, staying short of the painful angle30–45 s × 5, dailyStairs hurt 1–2 points less and you can hold 45 seconds
2 · Add range and loadStep-downs (start from a 10 cm step) + seated knee extension (bodyweight first)3 × 8–12 each, every other dayThe lowering is controlled, the knee doesn't collapse inwards, and you stay in the green
3 · Back to lifeTwo-leg squats → single-leg load (split squats, step-ups) + aerobic work (cycling, brisk walking)3 × 8–12, 2–3× per weekThree flights of stairs and a 40-minute walk, pain-free
First-line treatment for knee osteoarthritis is exercise and, where relevant, weight loss — not painkillers and not arthroscopy. Arthroscopic surgery for degenerative meniscal tears has shown no added benefit over exercise therapy or placebo surgery in trials. Exercise feels counterintuitive — "use a worn joint more?" — but cartilage depends on regular loading for its nutrition.
Running does not wear out knees. Recreational runners have a lower prevalence of knee osteoarthritis than sedentary people. The real risk factors are previous joint injury, obesity and sudden jumps in training volume — not running itself.

Improvement in chronic pain is rarely "one day it stopped". It is more good days and shorter bad ones. Judging progress that way is far healthier than asking yourself every morning whether it still hurts.

🚩 See a clinician first — don't start here

General red flags: pain that began with trauma (a fall, a crash, a moment of injury) · fever or chills · unexplained weight loss · a history of cancer · night pain at rest that wakes you · pain that keeps worsening independent of activity · long-term steroid or immunosuppressant use.
Back-specific red flags: progressive weakness in the legs · numbness in the saddle area · loss of bladder or bowel control, or difficulty passing urine — these suggest cauda equina syndrome, which is an emergency. Go now.
Shoulder: inability to lift the arm at all after an injury (possible acute cuff tear or fracture) · visible deformity · numbness or weakness in the arm.
Knee: a hot, red, markedly swollen joint (infection or inflammatory arthritis) · instability, giving way or locking after an injury · inability to bear weight.
Any of the above: see a doctor before training. See Day 63 · Red Flags.

❓ FAQ

It hurts while I exercise. Should I keep going?
Follow the traffic light: 0–3 keep going and even add load; 4–5 hold; above 5, or not back to baseline by morning, reduce. In chronic pain rehab, "pain-free before you train" is not a requirement — held to that standard, many people would never start.
Should I get an X-ray or MRI?
With red flags, immediately. Without them, early imaging for chronic shoulder, back or knee pain doesn't improve outcomes, and because degeneration and tears are common in people with no symptoms, it often produces unnecessary worry and more unnecessary tests. The usual order: train properly for 6–12 weeks, and reassess if nothing moves.
Do steroid injections help?
Often in the short term — weeks of meaningful relief, sometimes opening a window in which rehab can start. Medium and long term they are not superior to conservative care, and repeated injections may be unkind to tendon and cartilage. Treat them as something that makes training possible, not as the treatment. Whether one suits you is a clinical decision.
Can I take painkillers long term?
Paracetamol is close to ineffective for chronic low back pain; NSAIDs help modestly short term but carry gastrointestinal, kidney and cardiovascular risk with prolonged use; opioids are not recommended for chronic non-cancer pain — unclear long-term benefit against clear dependence risk. Leave the regimen to a clinician; see Day 50.
How long until it gets better?
Think in months. Most people notice functional improvement at 6–12 weeks, but chronic pain moves in waves — flare-ups are part of the process, not failure. What predicts the long-term outcome is whether you kept training, not how many points it dropped in the first fortnight.
All three areas hurt. Can I do everything at once?
You can, but don't run three full modules together. Pick the one that most affects your life and do it fully, keeping only stage 1 (isometrics / walking) for the other two. Once the first reaches stage 3, bring the second up. Pushing three at once almost guarantees overdoing it.
