TRAINING PROGRAM

Meditation Starter Plan
4 Weeks · 10 Minutes a Day

4 weeks · 10 min daily · no cross-legged sitting, no app, no belief required · plus a goal-directed menu, the brahmavihārās and Yoga Nidra
This plan's stance: meditation genuinely works, at a small-to-moderate effect size: there is moderate evidence for anxiety, depression and pain, but no clear advantage over active comparisons like exercise or relaxation training. Worth practising, then — not a cure-all. This plan also says nothing about enlightenment. It trains one concrete, observable skill: noticing that your mind has wandered, and bringing it back. That move is the entire practice; everything else is seating arrangements.
HOW TO USE
First, three misunderstandings — then how the 10 minutes split
Three premises to correct
You assumeActually
You have to empty your mindYou don't. Thoughts keep coming — that is a brain working normally. Notice the wandering → come back: that is one repetition. More wandering means more reps
You have to sit cross-leggedYou don't. A chair, back supported, feet on the floor is fine. The only requirement is that you can stay still for 10 minutes without dozing off
It is religious or mysticalIt doesn't have to be. What follows is the secular attention-training version — the same one used in clinical research
The daily 10 minutes (three fixed blocks)
BlockWhatTime
① SettleSit, set a timer for 8 minutes, take 3 slow exhales to let the body stop1 min
② PracticeThat week's method (the four cards below)8 min
③ CloseOpen your eyes; don't grade how it went. Just note: today I sat1 min
When matters more than how long. Attach it to something you already do daily — after brushing your teeth, after pouring coffee, once you're in the car — and adherence beats "whenever I have time" by a wide margin. Morning usually beats evening, when it tends to become sleep.

WEEK 1 · ANCHOR
Week 1 · The breath anchor (this week decides whether you continue)
Practice · 8 min
StepHow
1. Pick an anchorCool air at the nostrils, or the rise and fall of the belly — pick one and stop switching. If sensation is faint, rest a hand on the belly and use touch
2. StayDon't steer the rhythm, just feel it. Breathing training adjusts actively; meditation watches passively — don't blend the two
3. DriftYou will. The moment you notice, say "thinking" quietly inside, and return to the anchor
4. No gradingSkip "I drifted again, I'm bad at this". That judgement is simply the next thought — bring it back the same way
Success this week is not "how long I stayed focused" but "how many times I came back". Drifting 30 times in 8 minutes and returning 30 times is a good session — that's 30 reps.
Days 3–5 are where people quit: novelty gone, benefit not yet arrived. On those days let yourself sit for 3 minutes, but don't break the chain. Days practised matters far more than minutes per sit.
WEEK 2 · BODY
Week 2 · Body scan
Practice · 8 min
StepHowTime
Start at the feetAttention to the toes and soles — what is actually there right now (warmth, pressure, tingling, or nothing; "nothing" is also an answer)~1 min
Move up slowlyShins → thighs → pelvis → abdomen and back → chest → hands → neck and shoulders → face → crown, 20–40 s each~6 min
Whole bodySpend the last minute sensing the body as one thing1 min
Most people notice something for the first time this week: the shoulders have been up, the jaw has been clenched, the breath has been stuck high in the chest. That is the body scan's practical value — it turns background tension you never registered into something visible, and therefore something you can put down.
Lying down invites sleep. Falling asleep isn't failure, but if it happens every time, switch to sitting or move the practice to the morning.
WEEK 3 · THOUGHTS
Week 3 · Watching thoughts (noting)
Practice · 8 min
The breath stays in the background, but this week you stop shooing thoughts away. When one arrives, give it the simplest possible label, then return to the breath:
What showed upSay inside
Thinking about tomorrow's meeting"planning"
Replaying something that was said"remembering"
Worrying about something"worrying"
Discomfort somewhere in the body"sensation"
Judging how badly this is going"judging"
Labelling isn't taxonomy, it's a change of vantage point: from "I am worrying" to "a worrying thought has appeared". That small gap is the mechanism by which meditation actually helps with anxiety — not by removing thoughts, but by removing the obligation to follow every one.
Keep labels light and short. If you catch yourself deliberating whether something counts as planning or worrying, that is already new thinking — pick either and go back to the breath.
WEEK 4 · INTO THE DAY
Week 4 · Open awareness, and the part that needs no sitting
Formal practice · 8 min: open awareness
This week the breath stops being the sole target. No assigned anchor — attention rests on whatever is most prominent right now: a sound, a spot in the body, a thought. It will move on its own; you simply know that it moved. This is harder than the first three weeks, which is why it comes last. If it scatters, drop back to the breath anchor at any point — that is not a regression.

TARGETED

LOVING-KINDNESS · METTA
Loving-kindness · treating goodwill as a movement you can train
How to do it (one round, 10–15 min)
StepWhatTime
1 · SettleSit, three slow exhales. A hand on the chest is allowed — the gesture is soothing in itself1 min
2 · Fix the phrasesChoose four and don't change them mid-round: May you be safe · May you be healthy · May you be at ease · May your life go well—
3 · Widen the circleHold one specific person in mind, repeat the four phrases silently, then move to the next. Order below2–3 min each
4 · CloseDrop the phrases and sit for a moment doing nothing1 min
The order of circles: a benefactor (or anyone easy to feel warmth towards — a child, a pet) → yourself → someone close → a neutral person (the shop assistant, a neighbour) → someone mildly difficult → all beings.
The traditional order starts with yourself — I'd suggest you don't. In clinical practice many people find self-directed phrases awkward or actively aversive, particularly those high in self-criticism. Start with whoever is easiest to feel warmth for, and turn towards yourself once the warmth is actually there. The success rate is much higher.
Where nearly everyone gets stuck
What happensWhat to do
Nothing felt — it's like reciting linesNormal. What you repeat is the intention; you are not manufacturing a feeling. Warmth is a by-product, not a pass mark — the flat sessions count too
Saying it to yourself feels falseSwitch to the third person (use your own name), or stay with the benefactor circle for a fortnight before turning inward
Old grief surfaces partway throughA common backdraft in self-compassion work. Shorten it, go back to the breath, don't push through. If it keeps happening, pause and talk to someone
The phrases won't come for a "difficult person"Skip them. Begin with someone mildly difficult (a colleague you disagree with), never someone who harmed you
You get angrier the longer you practiseStop. Loving-kindness is not a lid for anger, and anger is often legitimate and informative
Loving-kindness is not forgiveness, not reconciliation, and certainly not a requirement to return to a harmful relationship. It trains your internal capacity to summon goodwill — it does not endorse anyone's behaviour. Any version of "you should feel loving-kindness towards them" offered as a reason to keep enduring something is a misuse of it.
Three ways to use it without sitting down
SituationHow
Queuing, waiting for a lift, on the trainPick any stranger in view and silently offer one phrase. Sixty seconds, entirely invisible
Walking to workWiden the circles as you go — change person at each red light
Just after an argumentYou can direct it at that person, but do your own circle first — the other order usually turns into self-blame
How far the evidence goes
In meta-analyses, loving-kindness meditation shows a roughly moderate effect on positive emotion and smaller effects on depression and anxiety; there is experimental evidence for prosocial behaviour (willingness to help). The typical dose studied is 10–15 minutes daily for 6–8 weeks. As with focused attention, it is an addition, not a replacement for treatment. For people with a trauma history, severe depression or intense self-criticism, the self-directed segment can provoke distress — see the safety note at the end of this page.
BRAHMAVIHĀRĀS
Joy and equanimity · loving-kindness is the first of four

Traditionally, loving-kindness isn't a standalone practice — it is the first of the four brahmavihārās. Each targets one specific emotional difficulty, and only together do they make a complete set:

WhichTargetsIn one lineWhere on this page
Mettā · loving-kindnessColdness, hostility, harshness towards yourselfMay you be wellPrevious card
Karuṇā · compassionTurning away from suffering, or burning out on itI see your painCompassion meditation in the menu above

Muditā · sympathetic joy (for envy and comparison)
This is the most neglected of the four, and what it targets is close to universal now: that tightening in the chest when a peer's promotion, house or result scrolls past. The reason it deserves its own practice is that almost nothing else in psychology aims directly at envy. CBT works on the thought, mindfulness observes the moment; sympathetic joy trains the movement of being glad for someone else.
StepWhat
The four phrasesMay your good fortune continue · May your happiness grow · May your effort be seen · I am glad for you

Where you get stuckWhat to do
The phrases won't come for that personStep back a circle; don't force it. Envy isn't a moral failing, it's a signal — it usually points accurately at something you want, which is worth thinking about on its own
It turns into self-reproach ("why am I so petty")That's mettā's job, not muditā's. Do a round towards yourself, then come back

TRY IT
The best use of this one isn't on a cushion: while scrolling, when a post makes you tighten, offer that person one phrase on the spot. Ten seconds. More useful than closing the app, because the tightening itself is exactly what you're training.
Upekkhā · equanimity (for over-involvement and empathy burnout)
Equanimity is compassion's safety valve. Compassion without it is empathy burnout — carers, clinicians, and anyone who lies awake all night because a friend is upset are usually not short on empathy. They're short on this.
One misunderstanding first: equanimity is not coldness, not indifference, and not "let them get on with it". It is caring without taking the other person's emotion as your own — and that distinction is the difference between still caring in a year and being hollowed out in three months.
StepWhat
1Bring to mind someone you worry about too much
2Four phrases: May you be safe · May you be happy · This is not in my hands · I care about you, and your path is yours to walk
3On the third phrase, check your body — did the chest or the shoulders let go slightly? If so, it is working
Dose5–8 minutes
If "this is not in my hands" feels like shirking, stop and separate two things: what you can actually do for them, and what you are only anxiously carrying on their behalf. Go do the first. The second is what this practice is for putting down.
How far the evidence goes
Mettā and compassion have meta-analytic support (moderate effects on positive emotion, experimental evidence for prosocial behaviour). Muditā and upekkhā have essentially no standalone randomised trials — they are mostly studied as components of compassion-training courses, so no separate effect size can be given. They are here because what they target is specific, the method is clear, and there is no known risk — not because the evidence matches loving-kindness.
YOGA NIDRA · LYING DOWN
Yoga Nidra / NSDR · the practice that lets you fall asleep
The core difference from a body scan: the body scan trains awareness and dozing off is a miss; Yoga Nidra aims at physiological recovery, and falling asleep is not a failure. That single difference makes it the lowest-barrier practice on this page — no sitting, no getting good at it, no pass mark. NSDR (non-sleep deep rest) is the secularised name for much the same thing.
StepHow
Lie downOn your back, a pillow under the knees, and cover up — a dropping body temperature pulls you back out of it
Set an intentionOne short sentence. You're not trying to make it happen, just placing it there
Tour the bodyFollow the guidance quickly through body parts — much faster than a body scan, no dwelling, no analysis
Breath and contrastCount breaths downwards, or alternate sensing cold and warm, heavy and light
Come backSlowly; roll to your side before sitting up, and don't spring to your feet
When to use itLength
The afternoon dip (an alternative to a nap that doesn't cost you the night)20 min
Awake at night and not getting back (don't check the time, just start)10–20 min
After a hard training day or a shift20 min
How far the evidence goes: there are randomised trials for sleep quality and anxiety with small-to-moderate effects, but study quality is uneven and samples are generally small. The line going round that "20 minutes of Yoga Nidra equals 2 hours of sleep" has no basis. The defensible claim is narrower: it relaxes people more than lying there on a phone, and it asks almost nothing of them.
Who it suits best: people who want to try meditation but get agitated the moment they sit down. For them this is often the only door that opens — start here, and leave seated practice for later.
TRACK · RETEST
What to log, and what not to
Log thisWhy
Sat / didn't sit (a tick is enough)The variable that predicts long-term benefit is frequency. At four weeks, count the ticks — not which sit went well
A rough sense of "how many times I came back"Keeps reminding you that drifting is the practice, not the failure
Weekly: a 0–10 rating of "reactivity"When something provokes you, has the gap between impulse and response widened? That is the change people report most
Don't log these: "depth" of the state, minutes of blankness, or an unbroken streak (one missed day makes far too many people abandon the whole thing). And ignore a wearable's "mindfulness score" — it measures heart rate changes, not attention.

⚠️ Safety & Myths

Myth 1: "Meditation means a blank mind." — It doesn't. Thoughts keep arriving; what is trained is noticing and returning. People who aim at blankness almost all decide within two weeks that meditation isn't for them.
Myth 2: "Practise and you stop feeling anxious or angry." — Emotions still happen. What changes is whether there is a gap in which you can choose a response — not the disappearance of the emotion.
Myth 3: "Longer is better." — The best-studied dose is 20–45 minutes a day in an 8-week mindfulness course, but for a beginner, 10 minutes daily for four weeks is worth far more than one hour-long sit followed by quitting.
Myth 4: "Meditation has no side effects." — It does. Pooled studies find around 8% of practitioners report adverse experiences, including increased anxiety, mood swings and feelings of dissociation. Uncommon, but real.
Do this with professional guidance if you have: PTSD or a significant trauma history (body scans can trigger flashbacks), an acute depressive or psychotic episode, or a history of dissociative symptoms. If practice is followed by hours of anxiety, unreality or worsened sleep, stop, switch to exercise or relaxation training, and talk to a professional. Meditation does not replace treatment (see Day 22).

❓ FAQ

My mind races the moment I sit. Am I just not suited to this?
That is everyone's starting state, not a disqualification. "Thinking non-stop" means you are seeing what has been happening all along unnoticed — which is what the practice starting to work looks like. There is only one thing to do: notice, come back.
App or no app?
Guided audio makes starting easier, and mindfulness apps do have randomised trial support, just with modest effect sizes. Use guidance for the first two weeks, then try going bare (timer only) from week three — the aim is to be able to sit without any external voice.
How is this different from breathing training? Which should I do?
Breathing training actively regulates physiological state and works within minutes; meditation passively observes and trains attention and your relationship to thoughts — slower, but a different layer. If you want a switch that brings you down now, start with breathing (see the Breathing Training Plan). If you want to change how you react to emotion, meditate. They stack: 2 minutes of slow breathing to settle, then 8 minutes of practice.
How long until I notice anything?
"Quieter while sitting" within days; "half a beat slower to react when provoked" usually 3–6 weeks; measurable change on sleep and mood scales mostly shows up in studies of 8-week courses. Four weeks is the on-ramp, not the destination.
Can it replace exercise or treatment?
No. For depression and anxiety, meditation performs comparably to exercise and relaxation training rather than better, and the evidence is not strong enough to substitute for medication or CBT. Treat it as one item in the mix, not a replacement.
